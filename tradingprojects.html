Vanguard ETF Pairs Trading Research

A Python research pipeline built for the 2026 Vanguard ETF Trading Challenge. It screens every active Vanguard ETF for pairs whose price ratio reliably reverts, then backtests a long-only rotation between the two funds in each pair. All output is PDF reports; nothing connects to a broker or places orders.

The distinction driving the design: correlation says two funds move together, cointegration says the gap between them comes back. Only the second is tradable, and the two rarely agree — the most correlated pairs in this universe are usually the least cointegrated, because near-identical index funds drift apart without ever being pulled back.

Method:

  • Data: about 25 years of daily prices for 116 active Vanguard ETFs from Yahoo Finance (yfinance), cached locally once per day.
  • Ranking: pairs ranked by the correlation of daily returns (not price levels) over a 252-trading-day lookback. Two funds that both trend up correlate at 0.99 on price levels whether or not they have anything to do with each other.
  • Cointegration screen: Engle–Granger test on price levels. The test is not symmetric, so it runs in both directions and the weaker of the two p-values is the one reported; a pair passes only if that value is below 0.05. Pairs need at least 200 shared trading days to be tested at all.
  • Signal: z-score of the price ratio A/B against its 100-day rolling mean. Below −2, buy A; above +2, buy B.
  • Backtest: long-only (no shorting). Three exit rules are compared on identical entries (z = 0, ±1, ±2), and signalling pairs are combined into an equally weighted portfolio, rebalanced daily, against a buy-and-hold benchmark.
  • Caching: the day's download and the most recent cointegration screen are both cached on disk, so a repeat run skips the downloads and the hundreds of regressions behind the screen.

Reports:

  • Correlation report: the most correlated pairs by daily return, each with its rolling correlation across its full shared history, so a relationship that is drifting is visible rather than averaged away.
  • Cointegration screen: every ranked pair tested, ordered by significance rather than correlation. Pairs that pass get their prices, test statistics and signal history charted.
  • Portfolio report: opens with what is actionable today — the live signals and where each candidate sits relative to its trigger — then the backtest under each exit rule, the portfolio equity curve against buy-and-hold, and a page per pair.

Folder contents:

  • RunDaily.py: runs the full pipeline in order, each stage fed by the one before it; the usual entry point.
  • Vanguard.py: the ETF universe of 116 tickers and the data layer (download, daily cache, column extraction, gap reporting).
  • VanguardStats.py: aligned price/return tables and the correlation matrices, on levels and on returns.
  • VanguardReporter.py: correlation report (most correlated pairs and their rolling correlation).
  • VanguardPairsTrading.py: the cointegration screen, ratio z-scores and signal counts.
  • VanguardPairsPortfolio.py: today's live signals, the backtest under each exit rule, and the combined portfolio.
  • VanguardPairReport.py: a report for one pair on demand, e.g. python VanguardPairReport.py BIV VGIT. It refuses a pair that isn't cointegrated.
  • VanguardPlots.py: rolling-correlation charts used by the reports.
  • README.md: full documentation, configuration and runtime notes.

How to run:

Install with pip install pandas numpy yfinance matplotlib statsmodels scipy, then run python RunDaily.py. Requires Python 3.10+ (built and tested on 3.14). A full run takes under a minute after the first download; screening many more than the default 150 pairs is what makes it slower, since each pair costs two regressions.

Limitations:

  • No transaction costs, spreads, slippage or taxes; every return is an upper bound, and the strategy trades often enough for that to matter.
  • Multiple testing: screening many pairs at p < 0.05 produces false positives by chance; reported p-values are uncorrected.
  • The rolling window supplies the mean reversion: re-centering every 100 days makes even a non-stationary ratio produce plausible entries, so the cointegration test is the only thing separating signal from noise.
  • Adjusted prices are not traded prices. Adjusted close restates history at every dividend, which can flip a cointegration verdict for bond funds, where most of the return is income.
  • Survivorship bias: the universe is currently active ETFs only.
  • Long-only: without shorting, each position carries the market risk of the fund it holds rather than the spread.
